Glaucoma is a leading cause of vision loss that occurs when elevated intraocular pressure (from fluid buildup) reduces blood flow to the optic nerve, damaging it and destroying retinal ganglion cells. Vision loss typically starts in the peripheral field and becomes permanent without treatment.

Key factors include:

Type of glaucoma (open-angle, angle-closure, normal-tension, or secondary)

Risk factors such as age over 60, family history, diabetes, and high eye pressure

Early warning signs like blurred vision, eye pain, halos around lights, or gradual side-vision loss

Treatments including prescription eye drops, laser therapy, and surgery to lower pressure and slow progression

Glaucoma is a group of eye conditions that damage the optic nerve, the crucial link between your eye and brain. Over time, this damage can lead to permanent glaucoma vision loss if left untreated. Understanding how glaucoma develops and progresses can help you recognize risks early, seek timely care, and protect your sight.

1. Types of Glaucoma

There are several forms of glaucoma, but the two most common are:

Most prevalent in Western countries

Develops slowly, often without noticeable symptoms

Drainage canals in the eye gradually become less efficient

Less common but can progress rapidly

The iris bulges forward, blocking fluid drainage

May cause sudden eye pain, headache, nausea, blurred vision

Both types share the same end result: increased pressure within the eye (intraocular pressure, or IOP) that harms the optic nerve.

2. Fluid Dynamics and Increased Eye Pressure

Your eye constantly produces a clear fluid called aqueous humor. This fluid:

Nourishes internal eye structures

Exits through a drainage system (the trabecular meshwork and Schlemm's canal)

When production outpaces drainage, fluid builds up, raising IOP. Elevated IOP is a major risk factor for glaucoma vision loss, but damage can also occur at "normal" pressures in some people.

Key Points on Eye Pressure

Normal IOP range: 12–22 mm Hg

Sustained pressures above this range increase stress on optic nerve fibers

4. Cellular and Molecular Responses

Inside the retina and optic nerve, several processes drive glaucoma vision loss:

Programmed cell death triggered by stress signals

Involves elevated glutamate, calcium influx, and mitochondrial dysfunction

Activation of glial cells (astrocytes and microglia)

Release of inflammatory cytokines that exacerbate nerve injury

Accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

Damages cellular membranes, proteins, and DNA (Weinreb et al., 2014)

These molecular events are ongoing, which is why glaucoma is a chronic, progressive disease.

5. Structural Changes: Optic Disc Cupping

As RGC axons die off, the optic nerve head undergoes noticeable structural changes:

The cup-to-disc ratio increases

"Cupping" appears as an enlarged, excavation-like area

Progressive cupping correlates with worsening visual field defects

Eye doctors monitor the optic disc during routine exams to detect early signs of glaucomatous damage.

6. Patterns of Vision Loss

Glaucoma typically affects peripheral vision first, and central vision is involved later. Common patterns include:

Paracentral scotomas: small blind spots near the center of vision

Arcuate defects: curved areas of vision loss following nerve fiber paths

Tunnel vision: advanced narrowing of the visual field, leaving only central vision

Because early peripheral changes often go unnoticed, glaucoma is called the "silent thief of sight."

8. Symptoms and When to Seek Help

Early glaucoma may present with no symptoms. As damage progresses, you may notice:

Gradual loss of peripheral vision

Blurred vision or difficulty focusing

Halos around lights (more common in angle-closure glaucoma)

Eye pain, redness, headache, nausea (angle-closure)

If you experience sudden eye pain, severe headache, nausea, or rapid vision changes, get immediate help—these could be signs of an Acute Glaucoma Attack, a medical emergency that requires urgent evaluation and treatment.

9. Diagnosis and Monitoring

Eye care professionals use several tests to diagnose and track glaucoma:

Tonometry: measures intraocular pressure

Ophthalmoscopy: examines the optic nerve for cupping

Visual field testing: maps peripheral vision defects

Optical coherence tomography (OCT): images retinal nerve fiber layer thickness

Early detection allows for interventions that slow or prevent further glaucoma vision loss.

10. Treatment to Preserve Vision

While lost vision cannot be restored, treatment focuses on lowering IOP and protecting the optic nerve:

Medications (eye drops): prostaglandin analogs, beta-blockers, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors

Laser therapy: trabeculoplasty to improve fluid drainage

Surgery: trabeculectomy or drainage devices to create new outflow pathways

Adherence to treatment and regular follow-up are essential for long-term protection.

11. Living with Glaucoma

Managing glaucoma involves lifestyle adjustments and ongoing care:

Take medications exactly as prescribed

Attend regular eye exams (every 3–12 months, depending on severity)

Maintain a healthy diet and exercise routine

Avoid activities that dramatically raise eye pressure (e.g., inverted yoga poses)
