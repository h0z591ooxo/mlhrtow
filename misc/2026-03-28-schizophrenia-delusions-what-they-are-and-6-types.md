Types of Schizophrenia Delusions

Delusions can fall into two categories: bizarre and non-bizarre delusions, says George Keepers, MD, the Elder V. Carruthers Professor of Psychiatry at Oregon Health and Science University in Portland.

Bizarre delusions involve beliefs that are impossible, not understandable, and not rooted in reality — for instance: “The idea that somebody’s brain could be removed without any scars showing on the scalp is impossible,” says Dr. Keepers.

Non-bizarre delusions include situations that could happen in real life, such as being followed by someone or being cheated on, but are untrue or highly exaggerated. For example, a person having a non-bizarre delusion might believe without evidence that they’re being followed or are under surveillance by the police, says Keepers.

There are several different types of bizarre and non-bizarre delusions that fall into the following categories.

Persecutory Delusions

“These are beliefs that groups or people are looking to harm you in some way, that you’re being targeted in some way,” Dr. Gonzales says.

Grandiose Delusions

People with grandiose delusions may have an overinflated sense of self-worth. “Grandiose beliefs have to do with thinking you’re special in some way, maybe you have special powers or you’re secretly wealthy or you’ve been tasked with a secret mission, “ Gonzales says.

Referential Delusions

Referential delusions include beliefs that ordinary events, places, or objects hold major personal significance in your life even though there’s no logical connection to you, says Gonzales. For example, someone having a referential delusion may see someone staring at them on the subway and assume that person is trying to send them a signal.

“You might hear something on the radio and think they’re talking directly to you or see something on TV and think it’s a direct message to you,” she adds.

Erotomanic Delusions

Erotomanic delusions, also known as de Clérambault's syndrome, often involve an individual believing without evidence that another person — typically a celebrity or someone high-profile — is madly in love with them.

“You might think you’re in a relationship with someone or that they’re in love with you when they’re not,” Gonzales says. “They may not even know you at all. It doesn’t have to be with a celebrity, and it could be just someone who is in your life.”

Control and Thought Broadcasting Delusions

“These are more what’s called passive categories of beliefs,” says Gonzales. “Things like thought withdrawal where people are stealing your thoughts or inserting thoughts into your mind so your thoughts aren’t your own anymore.”

“Some people might even believe other people can hear what you’re thinking,” she adds.

How to Manage Schizophrenia Delusions

Experiencing delusions can be a traumatic experience, says the adult and child psychiatrist Christine Crawford, MD, MPH, an assistant professor of psychiatry at Boston University School of Medicine in Massachusetts and an associate medical director at the National Alliance on Mental Illness (NAMI).

“[Delusions] can be quite distressing for the individual who is living with schizophrenia because they truly believe that someone is out for them, or that something is happening, even though people are trying to explain to them that’s actually not the case,” she says.

“It’s really hard and invalidating because you truly believe this is your reality,” adds Gonzales. “People can withdraw and isolate themselves from people, which makes them feel worse. It can be a vicious cycle.”

Antipsychotic Medication “The mainstay of treatment for all symptoms is antipsychotic treatment. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Psychosis (CBTp) Like conventional cognitive behavioral therapy, CBTp teaches patients to work through their thought processes, find new ways of thinking, and learn coping strategies for when they encounter triggers or have feelings of anxiety and discomfort, Gonzales says. How to Help a Loved One With Delusions

It can be challenging to know how to help a loved one who’s experiencing delusions. Here are some tips that can help.

1. Don’t Try to Convince Your Loved One That the Delusions Aren’t Real

While it may seem intuitive to tell a loved one their delusions aren’t real, this is actually counterproductive, Gonzales says. “If you’re a family member who’s tried this, you’ll already know from experience — it’ll turn into an argument, the person will shut down, and it might be potentially damaging for your relationship,” she says. While their delusions aren’t based in reality, to them it is their reality, Gonzales adds.

Some family members may worry that they might strengthen their loved one’s delusions if they don’t challenge them, Gonzales says. This isn’t the case. As long as you aren’t adding more information to support their delusion, you’re not going to risk worsening them, she notes.

2. Instead, Try to Empathize With Your Loved One

Try to show your loved one empathy for how stressed and concerned they may be feeling about the delusions they’re having. For instance, you might consider telling your loved one they seem distracted and less engaged with the present moment instead of pointing out that they haven’t been taking care of themselves or are always late to work.

It’s an incredibly isolating experience when your loved ones don’t believe in the reality you’re experiencing, Dr. Crawford says. “A lot of people feel somewhat invisible in this process and they don’t realize other people are noticing them or care about them,” she says.

It’s important to be realistic about when and how much you can intervene with what your loved one is experiencing.

In some rare instances, the delusions someone has may be related to their family members, which can create an unsafe space in the home, Keepers says. If this is the case for your loved one, you should prioritize your own safety, keep your distance if needed, and seek help from their doctor or a mental health professional for handling the situation.

Again, don’t try to convince your loved one that their reality is false, as this could worsen the situation. Instead, focus on maintaining a positive relationship, even if it’s from a distance, he says.

Also, consider how staunch your loved one is in their beliefs. “If they’re having doubts, then it’s okay to discuss with them what those doubts are, why they feel the belief might not be right, and try to help them with alternate explanations,” Keepers says.

The Takeaway

Delusions are a very common symptom of schizophrenia, affecting up to 90 percent of those with the condition.

These steadfast beliefs aren’t based in reality but are very distressing, and can range from thoughts of being followed or attacked to the idea that their thoughts are being broadcast to the public.

Symptoms of schizophrenia like delusions can be treated through a combination of antipsychotic medication and cognitive behavioral therapy for psychosis.
