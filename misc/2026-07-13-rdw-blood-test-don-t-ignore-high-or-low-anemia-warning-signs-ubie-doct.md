Understanding Your RDW Blood Test: What High or Low Values Mean for Anemia

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

The RDW blood test measures variation in red blood cell size. A high RDW often signals iron, B12, or folate deficiency, mixed anemia, or recovery after blood loss, while a low RDW is generally not concerning. RDW results must be interpreted alongside MCV, hemoglobin, symptoms, and follow-up tests to identify the specific type of anemia.

Key takeaways:

High RDW + low MCV: often iron deficiency anemia

High RDW + high MCV: possible B12 or folate deficiency

High RDW + normal MCV: early nutrient deficiency or mixed anemia

Low RDW: usually not clinically significant

Explanation

If you've recently had blood work done, you may have noticed a measurement called RDW on your lab report. The rdw blood test is commonly included as part of a complete blood count (CBC), and it can provide important clues about your red blood cells and whether anemia may be present.

Seeing numbers outside the "normal" range can be unsettling. The good news is that RDW is just one piece of the puzzle. Understanding what it measures—and what high or low values might mean—can help you have a more informed discussion with your doctor.

What Is the RDW Blood Test?

RDW stands for Red Cell Distribution Width. It measures the variation in size of your red blood cells.

Red blood cells carry oxygen from your lungs to the rest of your body.

In healthy individuals, most red blood cells are similar in size.

RDW tells your doctor how much your red blood cells differ in size from one another.

The result is usually reported as a percentage. In most laboratories, a typical reference range is about 11% to 15%, though ranges may vary slightly.

Why Is RDW Important?

The rdw blood test is especially useful when evaluating different types of anemia, a condition in which your body does not have enough healthy red blood cells or hemoglobin to carry adequate oxygen.

Anemia is common and can develop for many reasons, including:

Iron deficiency

Vitamin B12 deficiency

Folate deficiency

Chronic disease

Blood loss

Genetic conditions such as thalassemia

RDW helps doctors distinguish between these causes when combined with other blood test values, particularly:

MCV (Mean Corpuscular Volume) – measures the average size of your red blood cells

Hemoglobin – measures oxygen-carrying protein

Hematocrit – measures the percentage of blood made up of red blood cells

RDW rarely tells the full story on its own. Instead, it provides context.

What Does a High RDW Mean?

A high RDW means there is a larger-than-normal variation in the size of your red blood cells. In simple terms, some cells are much bigger or smaller than others.

Common Causes of High RDW

Iron Deficiency Anemia

One of the most common causes.

Early iron deficiency often causes RDW to rise before other numbers change.

Cells may be smaller than normal (low MCV).

Vitamin B12 or Folate Deficiency

These deficiencies can produce larger red blood cells.

RDW increases as both large and normal cells circulate together.

Mixed Anemia

For example, someone with both iron and B12 deficiency.

Leads to a wide variation in cell size.

Recent Blood Loss or Recovery

When your body produces new red blood cells after bleeding, size variation may temporarily increase.

Chronic Illness or Inflammation

Some chronic diseases can affect red blood cell production and size.

A high RDW does not automatically mean something dangerous is happening. However, it does suggest that further evaluation may be needed to identify the underlying cause.

What Does a Low RDW Mean?

A low RDW is much less common and usually not a cause for concern on its own.

When RDW is low, it means your red blood cells are very uniform in size. In most cases:

A low RDW is considered clinically insignificant.

Doctors rarely use it alone to diagnose any condition.

If your RDW is slightly below the reference range but other values are normal, it often does not require further action.

How RDW and MCV Work Together

To better understand your rdw blood test result, doctors look at it alongside MCV. Here's how combinations can guide diagnosis:

1. High RDW + Low MCV

Often seen in:

Iron deficiency anemia

Some cases of thalassemia

2. High RDW + High MCV

Alcohol-related anemia

3. Normal RDW + Low MCV

May suggest:

Thalassemia trait

Anemia of chronic disease

4. Normal RDW + Normal MCV

If anemia is present, may point toward:

Acute blood loss

These patterns help narrow down the likely cause, but additional tests are usually needed.

Symptoms of Anemia to Watch For

If your rdw blood test suggests possible anemia, you may notice symptoms such as:

Fatigue or weakness

Shortness of breath

Pale skin

Dizziness or lightheadedness

Cold hands and feet

Headaches

Chest discomfort in severe cases

Some people have mild anemia and no symptoms at all. Others may feel significantly affected.

When Is RDW a Sign of Something Serious?

Most RDW changes are related to common and treatable conditions, especially nutritional deficiencies.

However, in rare cases, abnormal results may be linked to:

Severe chronic disease

Bone marrow disorders

Certain inherited blood conditions

If you experience any of the following, seek medical care promptly:

Severe shortness of breath

Chest pain

Fainting

Rapid or irregular heartbeat

Signs of significant blood loss (such as black or bloody stools)

These could signal a more serious condition that requires urgent evaluation.

If your rdw blood test is outside the normal range, your doctor may recommend:

Iron studies (ferritin, transferrin saturation)

Vitamin B12 and folate testing

Reticulocyte count (young red blood cells)

Additional evaluation for bleeding

In rare cases, referral to a hematologist

Treatment depends entirely on the cause. For example:

Iron deficiency is often treated with dietary changes and iron supplements.

Vitamin B12 deficiency may require oral supplements or injections.

Folate deficiency is treated with folic acid supplementation.

Chronic disease–related anemia focuses on managing the underlying condition.

Early treatment can significantly improve symptoms and prevent complications.

If RDW is elevated due to a nutritional deficiency, lifestyle changes may help alongside medical treatment:

Eat iron-rich foods (lean meats, beans, spinach, fortified cereals).

Include vitamin C to improve iron absorption.

Consume adequate B12 (animal products or fortified foods).

Ensure sufficient folate intake (leafy greens, legumes, citrus fruits).

However, supplementation should be guided by lab testing. Taking iron or vitamins unnecessarily can sometimes cause harm.

Key Takeaways About the RDW Blood Test

The rdw blood test measures variation in red blood cell size.

A high RDW often points toward iron, B12, or folate deficiency.

A low RDW is usually not clinically significant.

RDW must be interpreted alongside other CBC values.

Most causes of abnormal RDW are treatable.

Persistent symptoms or severe signs require prompt medical attention.

The Bottom Line

An abnormal RDW result can be concerning, but it is not a diagnosis by itself. It is a clue—one that helps your doctor better understand what may be happening in your body.
