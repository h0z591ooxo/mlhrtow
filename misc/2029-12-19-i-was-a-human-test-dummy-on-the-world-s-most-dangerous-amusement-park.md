# I Was a Human Test Dummy on the World's Most Dangerous Amusement Park Ride

Our product picks are editor-tested, expert-approved. We may earn a commission through links on our site. Why Trust Us?

Stories of the infamous Cannonball Loop at Action Park in Vernon, New Jersey abound. Stuff like the slide was designed on a napkin. Or that it was built from old, remnant pipes. Gene Mulvihill, the park's founder, apparently paid a test-rider $100 to try it. His son, Andy, even suited up in hockey gear to avoid being maimed on his test ride. They are all 100 percent true.

I started my twenty-three year tenure overseeing Action Park just a few weeks before its Memorial Day opening in May 1994. By then, the heyday (and the scandals) of the park were already behind us. Still, the loop lived on through stories, and for a time in the mid '90s, was even sporadically opened.

The Cannonball Loop, your average waterslide save for a terrifying vertical 360-degree loop in the middle, commonly stripped riders of their tops and bottoms upon exit, leaving a few bruises in its wake. People lined up all day long to be hosed down for lubrication and jump into the maw. Being spit out of the inversion was similar to that strangely attractive college drinking activity of running down a hallway, spinning around with your head on the grip end of a baseball bat, and then attempting to run back while bounding off the polished block walls.

In 1997, the park closed for a summer in bankruptcy, and following the purchase of the resort by a ski conglomerate, reopened the next year—without the Cannonball Loop. It was the end of an era, for the ride and for the park. Little did I know, years later, I'd have the chance to ride the loop once more and risk my life in the process.

By the fall of 2013, I'm still working at Action Park, now operating as Mountain Creek Waterpark and also owned again by the Mulvihill family (and Gene's son, Andy). We wanted to bring back some of what made Action Park memorable, without the obvious risks to our patrons' lives. While walking the million-square-foot international amusement park ride trade show in Orlando, Florida, I make a jaw-dropping discovery. Along one of the back rows reserved for smaller up-and-coming exhibitors, I recognize a huge tower, a fiberglass tube, and….a vertical 360-degree loop.

But it’s not our Cannonball Loop. It’s blue steel tubes inclined against a ski hill, a little different from our version. Almost tripping over myself, I ask “What is that?” “It’s the only 360-degree looping water ride in the world,” comes the polite understated Canadian guy behind the booth. “It’s never been done before." "Oh oh oh, you’re talking to the wrong guy!" I reply. "I hate to tell you, it’s been done before. We did it. It’s called the Cannonball Loop at Action Park, and it doesn’t really work. We tore it down."

Adrian, the Canadian, sheepishly admits that he knows about the Action Park loop, but argues that this loop is different. His partner, a legitimate engineer, woke up from a dream with an inspired solution to the 360-looping problem, and they’ve built a prototype. In minutes, Andy and I are at the booth together. He had also seen the Loop 2.0, and without too much formal discussion, we know instinctively that we must have it. This new loop at Action Park will boost attendance and re-establish our competitive position, safely, of course. What could be better than to reinvent the most visible and audacious icon of Action Park?

Here’s the rub: We can’t spend $1.5 million on a ride without testing this thing first. It’s November. We need to start building by March to complete the installation and certification by the time the park opens for the summer. Andy knows all too well the history of delivering new attractions late. It would drive Gene to paroxysms of fury and missed opportunity. So, we gotta go.

When we climbed out of the car to see the hulking structure above us in St. Louis, Missouri, Adrian took one look at Andy and his sturdily built body and immediately exclaimed, “You won’t fit. We used smaller diameter tubing for the prototype. Bill will have to test it." I remained standing, perhaps because all the blood had dropped to my feet. Cold feet.

Here’s how the engineer allegedly solved the issue of people getting stuck or otherwise injured in the Cannonball Loop: In the past, the problem was the variability of body weights. He eliminated that by building a capsule, a bullet if you will, that one would ride in, the diameter of the tubular cage riding just inside the diameter of the tubing. A bullet in the barrel of a gun. So with the inside of the barrel tube icing up, there was a good chance that the bullet, er, cage vessel wouldn’t make it through. Memories of the original Cannonball Loop and people stuck in the bottom came to mind.

“Hey, what kind of sales guy are you if you’ve never actually tried the product?” I shouted at Adrian. “I’m not going until you go." It took a few beats, but Adrian stepped up, paused for only a second, climbed into the cage, called to be pushed into the mouth of the tube, disappeared out of sight and…..did not emerge. The ice had frozen on the inside so much that the bullet was stuck in the barrel at the bottom of the loop.

The crew quickly unbolted the escape hatch and extricated Adrian, shaking from the cold, but otherwise no worse for wear. “Do we have a sale?” he asked. “No way,” said Andy. “We can’t buy this without proof it works. Bill, you’ve got to go, and now. We’re running out of light and time, and I like you, but I’m not spending Friday night in St. Louis with you.”

I’m in my old wetsuit and with the evaporative effect of the wind, it only seems to be a refrigerator now. In the water, I pee in my wetsuit because this stunt is now literally scaring the piss out of me. This is not something I should be doing at 52 years old. This is Johnny Knoxville territory. I’ll hit 50 MPH in a matter of seconds. I’ll feel the G-forces going through the loop. (I didn’t know then that I was also living with an aortic aneurysm.)

I look out over the expansive plains again in St. Louis. There is nothing to represent that there is a town here attached to the address we were given, other than the package store at the one four-way intersection that leads to what look like a scrap junkyard and factory. “If we needed emergency medical help, is there any?” I’m wondering. I watch while the factory crew assembles two high-reach forklifts with pressure washers hanging from them. They’ve turned off the circulating water and the lubrication jets. The plan is that just before I’m pushed into the tube, they’ll do a quick spray with the pressure washers and hope that the thin film of warm water doesn’t freeze before I pass through. Hope.

There’s no crew at the top with me now, just the inventor’s father and a cameraman from the Discovery Channel filming a documentary about the conception and realization of the world’s craziest new water ride. Everything is in place and I climb into the steel frame. I am belted with my arms firmly lashed across my chest so that I can’t reflexively stick my hands outside the frame. The cage is closed and bolted shut, squeezing my shoulders, and almost resting on my nose. There is no way for me to self-release this.

Suddenly, I know that this is all wrong, but everyone is ready to go. Andy has moved from the top of the tower to the bottom to give the launch OK and see me come out the exit. I know this is the future of our business, but I can’t do it. I start hyperventilating and shout “Get me out. Get me out." My throat is tight and my head is light. I reach for the cameramans’ tool belt and grab his Leatherman, quickly cutting and tearing at the tight wetsuit neoprene collar until I’m freely gulping air again.

I breathe deeply. I close my eyes and tell myself some nonsense about stepping up and doing what’s necessary. Rockets were built near here. Astronauts trusted them to escape the earth’s atmosphere and return unharmed. I just need to let gravity to do its thing, and it’ll be over in seconds. “Okay. Let’s go." Back into the cage for a second time I go. The camera guy fiddles with the Go-Pro attached inches from my face.

The only communication we have is a walkie talkie, and over the wind, it’s nearly impossible to hear. “F*ck it” I say firmly. “Push me now before I change my mind”. And so he does. The weightlessness of the freefall reminded me of my bungy jumping days, and the ride through the loop was smooth as silk. I knew I was upside down, and felt the momentary stall at the top of the loop, but it wasn’t uncomfortable. And so that’s what I said when I emerged out the chute. The cage was popped open, I turned to the camera and Andy to capture my reaction: “Yes! Andy! It’s fuckin' awesome! We gotta get this!”

I had done my job. For Andy, for the park, and for my own glorified sense of doing something that no one (well, only three of us) had ever done before. I had the film to prove it! Except I didn’t. In the rush of “Let’s go” moments before, the cameraman had pressed the ‘photo’ button instead of ‘video’ on the Go-Pro. All I had was a blurry shot of my entry into the tube.

I also had a mouthful of gycol-infused water and started to retch a bit as one of the factory crew reminded me of my failed manly shout inside the loop. “Sounded like a woman in there!” he laughed. High fives were shared all around, backs were slapped, and I shook a bit as I changed into dry clothes and we hit the road to race and make our flight back to Newark, New Jersey. I was still feeling the ill effects of the anti-freeze, so we stopped at a gas station deli where I decided that a massive dose of Minute Maid lemonade and a couple of packs of Keebler cheese crackers were the appropriate antidote.

I don’t remember much of the ride to the airport or the flight home, such was the long-lingering adrenaline rush of what had just happened, but I felt that maybe, just maybe, we’d found the next great Action Park attraction. Gene, deceased more than a year by that time, would be proud to see the park continue to flourish. Back in the office and over the following week and then months, our debate lingered and the clock ran out. In the end, resignedly, we realized that the mechanical cage nature of the loop-solution wasn’t true to the ethos of a park where “you’re in control of the action." The capital cost was also too risky for the business.

I wasn’t a Mulvihill. I wasn’t part of the original Action Park mythology during it’s explosive growth and controversy. But I felt the thrill of invention that powered the first hint of Action Park in 1974. I’m a sucker for the power of nostalgia, even the appropriated kind, and the importance of preserving stories that shape our culture and lives.

In a few years, Action Park will celebrate its 50th anniversary. The corners of the stories about the park will become rounded off and lose the personal passion with which they’re told. Action Park threatens to fade out as like an old rock 'n roll legend, diminishing the heartfelt and sometimes painful memories of a time in our youth when we felt we were freer to measure risk and make our own choices. I’d like to think that I did my job, measured the risks, enjoyed success and suffered the consequences, and delivered the best of Action Park, old and new.
