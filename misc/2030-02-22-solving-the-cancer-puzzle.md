Dr. Bryan Schneider seems much younger than his 36 years. Part of the reason, I suspect, stems from his boyish eagerness to share his research with me. He speaks and grins and leans in close. I find his manner infectious, and so I also lean forward. On a few occasions, our noses nearly rub.

"There are millions of genetic variations in the human body," he tells me, as if discovering this fact for the first time. "However, if just one of them mutates -- just one of them..." Dr. Schneider claps his hands. "Bang! "

He doesn't stop. "So in creating you, your parents provided two copies of each gene. This is the blueprint for every cell in your body. If the wrong combination comes together, you're at a higher risk of cancer. Now, there are also environmental exposures that increase the risk. Maybe the well water you drank as a child. Living near a smokestack. Cigarettes, of course. If you're genetically susceptible, environmental exposures can damage healthy cells. They start to look and act like cancer cells."

The IU Simon Cancer Center is also the home of the legendary Lawrence Einhorn, M. D., the oncologist who in 1974 actually cured one form of cancer -- "cure" being the operative term in a field that both dreads and covets the word. Dr. Einhorn's triumph was over testicular cancer, a pernicious disease that once killed 95 percent of all men (predominantly young men) in whom it had metastasized. But thanks to Dr. Einhorn and his team, Lance Armstrong is still racing, Nene Hilario is still playing NBA ball, Olympian Eric Shanteau still swims competitively, and, by Dr. Einhorn's own back-of-the- envelope calculation, 122,500 men once thought to be doomed have continued for the past 35 years to lead fruitful lives. No other form of cancer has been eradicated in such a dramatic manner.

Dr. Schneider, an assistant professor at Indiana University specializing in hematology/oncology, clinical pharmacology, and molecular genetics, would like to build on his mentor's success. Today we're seated in his small office on the fourth floor of the center, near the laboratory where he spends much of his workday.

As we've begun to unravel our genetic blueprint -- the human genome -- we're starting to have a better understanding of how this machinery works. Ultimately, biologics will tell us who will develop cancer and who will benefit from a specific drug.

"Right now, the tumors are still outsmarting us. But are we close to turning all types of cancer from fatal diseases into chronic diseases -- that is, diseases manageable enough that you will die of something else down the road? I believe we are close. Is that exciting or what?"

The 67-year-old Dr. Einhorn is still treating patients. A slight man with a crooked smile and thinning white hair, he looks like a cross between Woody Allen and Milton Friedman. Around his cluttered desk, the walls drip with testimonials, photos, and thank-you notes from survivors (average age 25). A rubber Livestrong bracelet is on his left wrist.

Cancer has been considered fatal since the dawn of organized medicine, Dr. Einhorn says. But it is only in the past two centuries that more-thorough autopsies, improved microscopes, and modern research science has determined that "cancer" is, in fact, "cancers."

Oncologists have long known that localized cancer cells do not kill. No woman dies from a lump in her breast. She dies because the mutated cells in her breast spread to her lungs or other vital organs. When the symptoms are caught early enough, these abnormal cells can be removed surgically or treated successfully with radiation and/or chemotherapy. Once the disease metastasizes, however, the odds of recovery plummet. In certain cancers (melanoma and pancreatic and esophageal cancers, for instance), those odds hover near zero.

Until Dr. Einhorn's dramatic discovery of the chemotherapy treatment cisplatin, which is derived from the heavy metal platinum, no one had been able to overcome the disease's inevitable lethality. Ever. Today, cancer trails only heart disease in the number of Americans it kills -- more than half a million in 2006. If you don't first suffer a fatal stroke or heart attack, or aren't hit by lightning, you will eventually die from one form of carcinoma or another. All it takes is one abnormal cell.

All this will change, however, as the medical community applies the information gleaned from the recently sequenced human genome. "That's the job for the next generation -- people like Dr. Schneider," Dr. Einhorn says. "There has been an explosion in knowledge of what we call the collective biology of cancer.

"Soon enough, sometime in this century, medicine will cease labeling cancers by the organs they affect, but instead by specific genes," he continues. "Mr. Smith may have lung cancer where gene number 18 is abnormal, whereas Mr. Jones may have lung cancer that looks the same under the microscope, but gene number 35 is abnormal. That's when we'll nail this thing. It's all about biologics."

Back in his office, Dr. Schneider explains that biologics are simply synthetic compounds, administered by IV or orally with tablets, built to disrupt a specific part of a cell's daily function that has gone haywire. They can be composed of a variety of substances, including antibodies -- which are usually made by the body to fight off infection. Once inside the body, biologics target fast-growing cancer cells exclusively, unlike the equal-opportunity, burn-'em-and- poison-'em methodology of radiation and chemotherapy.

The science of human genetics, Dr. Schneider reminds me, is still in its infancy. So naturally, the development of biologics remains in semi-conceptual stages. But some of these drugs have already shown great promise. The biologic Herceptin, for instance, was created in lab mice to recognize and attack breast-cancer cells. A recent study published in the British medical journal Lancet found that in one clinical trial, Herceptin significantly increased survival rates for 25 percent of women with a certain type of early breast cancer. The drug has unresolved side effects, including the danger of congestive heart failure, which affected 2 percent of patients in the trial. Nonetheless, it's an exciting advance for the more than 190,000 American women who'll be diagnosed with breast cancer this year. "And there are many more biologics in use or in development right now," Dr. Schneider says.

Rather than crowd out older radiation and chemotherapy techniques, these new drugs will "play nicely" with the standard treatments, Dr. Schneider says. In his view, managing cancer is the objective, much as a man with diabetes manages his illness. And the more we discover about the human genome..."Well, soon the treatments of not too long ago will be looked at as the dark ages of medicine.

"Someday, and this day is fast approaching," says Dr. Schneider, "we will figure out how to treat different cancers in different parts of the body with individual treatments based on each individual's genetic makeup."

Douglas Schwartzentruber, M. D., grimaces at my wordplay. "And if we do this right," he continues, "the discoveries coming from the genome will allow us -- and have already allowed us -- to approach cancer with molecularly targeted treatment."

Dr. Schwartzentruber, 52, knows this better than most. He recently concluded an 8-year clinical trial on a vaccine for metastatic melanoma, one of the deadliest cancers. The vaccine not only shrank melanoma tumors but delayed their spread. His is one of the first studies to prove that vaccines might have a medical benefit against cancer.

In May, Dr. Schwartzentruber presented his trial results at the annual American Society of Clinical Oncology convention. They became, appropriately, the talk of the conference. These were no small beans, given that the incidence of metastasized melanoma is among the fastest-increasing cancer rates in the United States. The American Cancer Society estimates that nearly 69,000 Americans will develop it this year; about 8,650 will die this year. Ninety percent of victims succumb within 5 years.

I've traveled 175 miles north from Indianapolis to the quaint farming town of Goshen to see Dr. Schwartzentruber, a surgical oncologist and the medical director of the 10-year-old Goshen Center for Cancer Care. The doctor is a tall, slender man with a thick Midwestern accent and the delicate hands of a concert pianist. Prior to returning here to his hometown 5 years ago to head the center, he served for 13 years as a senior investigator in the surgery branch of the National Institutes of Health's National Cancer Institute, in Bethesda, Maryland. He senses that I'm lost in the fog of medical terminology -- Gp100 protein? T cell receptors? -- and translates as best he can.

Take the word "vaccine," for instance. The shot I received as a kid? "Not quite," he says. "Most of the vaccines we're familiar with are given to healthy people to prevent infectious diseases -- polio and smallpox, for example. Here we're talking about a therapeutic vaccine, as opposed to a preventive one, that's administered to patients who already have metastatic melanoma. In layman's terms, what we're doing is gearing up the body's immune system -- supercharging it, so to speak -- to recognize and go after specific cancer cells. And the results are, well, heartening."

The biologic vaccine developed in Dr. Schwartzentruber's multi-institution study mimics a fragment, or marker, of a specific protein found on the surface of a melanoma-cancer cell. Administered alongside the immune-system-boosting therapy interleukin- 2 (another molecular biologic, also used to treat kidney cancer), the combination stimulates the body's white blood cells to seek and destroy that specific marker and therefore the cancer cell. Dr. Schwartzentruber's trial found that melanoma tumors treated solely with interleukin-2 shrank by 10 percent. When combined with the vaccine, the shrinkage more than doubled, to 22 percent. The patients lived an average of 20 weeks longer.

Despite this major advance, Dr. Schwartzentruber, like Dr. Schneider, isn't ready to walk away from traditional treatments such as radiation and chemotherapy. He, too, cites Dr. Einhorn's chemo work with testicular cancer as a motivating factor. "I remember thinking, Wow, we can cure one cancer; why not all?" And he mentions advances in diagnostic apparatus, from CT scans to MRIs to state-of-the-art PET scans, as keys to future eureka moments.

"Imaging has been one of the most significant advances in cancer care over the past 5 years," he says. "We have much better ways of spotting it early now."

He swivels in his chair to summon to his computer screen dueling images of a diseased kidney. The first is a two-dimensional computerized tomography, or CT, scan. The second is a three-dimensional positron emission tomography, or PET, scan.

"See here?" He moves a ballpoint pen across the screen to highlight the inflammation, which is much more clearly defined in the second image; it's like looking at a child's crayon drawing and then a draftsman's blueprint. "When we find the cancer early enough, this allows the radiation to really pinpoint and attack the problem, without what you might call collateral damage to healthy parts of the body, or even healthy parts of the same organ. Which in turn allows a patient to receive more radiation directly to the affected areas."

Dr. Schwartzentruber turns from the screen. "Unfortunately, all this stuff is expensive. One of the keys to beating this disease is finding ways to keep costs down. I don't have an answer for that."

Next for Dr. Schwartzentruber's team is a larger clinical trial. The goal: "Our challenge is figuring out why this vaccine is working only 22 percent and not 100 percent of the time." He reels off the names of a half dozen melanoma researchers, clinics, labs, and hospitals working with him toward that goal. "We've come this far, and we're all asking ourselves, 'What's next? How do we get to the next step?' These are all questions that fuel our research."

"That's a long way off , and I don't know if we will ever get to a totally preventive vaccine. But . . ." He pauses. "Can we alter the genes and prevent that cancer cell from mutating?" Dr. Kwon, a urologist and immunologist at the Mayo Clinic, specializes in cancer research. Earlier this year, his team announced what could be the Holy Grail of prostate cancer treatments: an immunity-boosting drug that eradicated nearly all vestiges of prostate cancer in four patients who had a metastasized form that was considered inoperable.

A layman's synthesis of Dr. Kwon's breakthrough goes like this: When certain immune cells rush to combat prostate cancer, the cancer cells have an innate ability to neutralize them. "Think of the cancer as being able to flick the 'off ' switch on these immune cells," Dr. Kwon says. Over the past 5 years, Dr. Kwon's team recruited 108 volunteers with advanced prostate cancer. Half the group was given standard hormone treatments, which are proved to temporarily slow but not eradicate prostate cancer. The other men were given the experimental biologic agent ipilimumab -- which prevents cancer cells from switching off the immune cells -- in addition to the hormone.

The four men whose cancers vanished were in the latter group. In one case, a golf-ball-size tumor invading the patient's bladder totally disappeared, and only microscopic deposits of cancer were left in his prostate -- making surgery an option again. Before the trial, these men had life expectancies of about 1 1/2 to 2 years. Afterward, they were pronounced cancer-free. (But not cured, of course.)

Dr. Kwon's biologic was developed in mice that had been genetically manipulated to produce human antibodies capable of stimulating an immune response to prostate cancer and other malignancies. Like Dr. Schwartzentruber's melanoma vaccine, and like the breast-cancer- fighting Herceptin, the experimental drug could not have been developed without the human genome. Your heart defies age. That's the amazing discovery Swedish scientists made earlier this year. Specifically, they found that heart cells regenerate at a rate of up to 1 percent a year. "It's a big idea," says Timothy Nelson, M.D., Ph.D., a Mayo Clinic researcher who studies heart disease. "The heart you're born with may not be the heart you die with." So what? Here's the bigger idea: If scientists can stimulate more heart-cell regeneration, they may help improve the heart's performance and repair damaged tissue. Stem cells may help do just that, according to Mayo Clinic studies. Dr. Nelson and his colleagues injected stem cells into mice that had recently had heart attacks, and the cells restored lost heart function and generated growth of new tissue. And mice embryos injected with stem cells -- and subjected to heart attacks after birth -- were also more likely to fend off heart failure. As many as 40 percent of all strokes have no easily identifiable cause. So research focuses more on treatment than prevention, says David Liebeskind, M.D., the associate neurology director at the UCLA Stroke Center. Two recent advances will help future stroke victims recover more quickly and completely. The first, a catheter called NeuroFlo, is inflated in the aorta and diverts more-oxygenated blood to the brain, says Dr. Liebeskind. If it's used within 12 hours of the onset of a stroke, it may prevent and even reverse brain damage. Clinical trials are just finishing, and results will be released next year. The second procedure is called external counterpulsation. Cuffs are placed around a patient's legs and inflated; this compresses the blood vessels, forcing oxygenated blood to the brain. In one small study, patients who underwent the treatment for 35 days showed greater improvement in motor skills, comprehension, and speech than stroke victims who didn't have the treatment. Next step: larger-scale testing.

"We've recently started testing an apparatus that manages diabetes more effectively than people can on their own," says Stuart Weinzimer, M.D., a Yale researcher studying the so-called closed-loop insulin pump. "It completely automates the process." The pump works like this: A sensor inserted under your skin monitors your blood sugar. When it rises, the sensor triggers the pump to deliver enough insulin to bring it back into a normal range. Study participants who used the device maintained ideal glucose levels 85 percent of the time, and kept their levels outside the danger zone at all times. Participants who used a traditional monitor maintained ideal levels 58 percent of the time, but their blood sugar entered the danger zone 7 percent of the time. The device is still being studied, says Dr. Weinzimer, who estimates that it could hit the market within 10 years.
