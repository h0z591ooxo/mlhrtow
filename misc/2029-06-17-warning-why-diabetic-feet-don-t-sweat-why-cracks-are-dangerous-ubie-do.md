Why Diabetic Feet Don't Sweat (and Why the Cracks Are Dangerous)

Dry skin on feet is a common problem in diabetes. When your feet don't sweat as they should, the skin becomes scaly, itchy, and prone to painful cracks. Left unchecked, these fissures can let bacteria and fungi enter, leading to infections that may require serious medical attention.

How Normal Feet Regulate Moisture

Sweat glands in your feet play an essential role in:

Keeping skin soft and supple

Preventing cracks and fissures

Helping regulate body temperature

Protecting against harmful microbes

When these glands work properly, they release moisture that forms a natural barrier. This barrier keeps your skin flexible and resistant to small injuries.

Why Diabetic Feet Don't Sweat

In diabetes—especially when blood sugar isn't well controlled—high glucose levels over time can damage small nerves. This condition is called diabetic autonomic neuropathy. When these nerves are affected:

Sweat glands receive fewer signals to release moisture

Blood flow to the skin is reduced

Skin cells become deprived of nutrients and oxygen

All of this leads directly to dry skin on feet. Diabetes also thins the skin's outer layers, making it less able to retain water.

Preventing and Managing Dry Skin on Feet in Diabetes

Taking good care of your feet every day is the best defense against cracks and infections.

Daily Foot Care Routine

Wash feet gently with lukewarm water; avoid hot water.

Use a mild soap—no harsh chemicals or fragrances.

Pat feet dry; don't rub, especially between the toes.

Apply a thick, fragrance-free moisturizer (lotion or cream) once or twice daily.

Avoid putting moisturizer between the toes to reduce fungal growth.

Footwear and Socks

Wear well-fitting shoes with enough room for toes.

Choose shoes with breathable materials to reduce sweat buildup.

Change socks daily; opt for moisture-wicking fabrics.

Avoid tight socks or elastic bands that restrict circulation.

Regular Inspections

Check your feet every day for cuts, cracks, redness, swelling, or blisters.

Use a mirror to view the soles, or ask someone to help.

Pay special attention to areas under bony prominences (heels, balls of feet).

When to Seek Professional Care

Cracks are deep, bleeding, or not improving within a few days.

Signs of infection: redness, warmth, swelling, pain, or pus.

Fever or unexplained chills accompany foot symptoms.

You have poor vision or limited ability to inspect your feet.

Lifestyle Tips for Healthier Feet

Control Blood Sugar: Aim for targets set by your healthcare team to slow nerve damage.

Stay Active: Gentle foot exercises improve circulation. Ankle rolls, toe curls, and walking are simple options.

Hydrate: Drinking enough water helps your entire body—including your skin—stay moisturized.

Quit Smoking: Tobacco further narrows blood vessels, worsening circulation.

When to Speak to a Doctor

Any sign of infection or non-healing wound in diabetes requires prompt medical attention. Speak to your doctor right away if you notice:

Spreading redness or swelling around a crack

Increasing pain, warmth, or throbbing in your foot

Fever or chills along with foot symptoms

A foot ulcer (an open sore that won't heal)

Your healthcare provider can prescribe topical or oral antibiotics, recommend specialty wound care, and adjust your diabetes treatment plan.
