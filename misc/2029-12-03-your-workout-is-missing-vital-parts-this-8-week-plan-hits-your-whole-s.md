# Your Workout Is Missing Vital Parts. This 8-Week Plan Hits Your Whole Strengthspan for More Gains.

THERE'S MORE THAN one type of strength. Someone who can deadlift 500 pounds has it in one key regard. But very often, they get it by neglecting other portions of the strength continuum. The result: You can be gym-"strong", but still get out of breath going up the stairs, or struggle to run and jump with your kids. Essentially, you're missing portions of the strength continuum.

“A few years ago I went all in on getting stronger, and yeah, that got me ‘gym strong.’ But other things—my explosion on the basketball court, my running—got just a bit weaker and a bit slower,” says Men’s Health fitness director Ebenezer Samuel, C.S.C.S.. “If you truly want to be a badass now and way into your 50s, 60s, and beyond, then your workouts have to build all of these kinds of strength—not just gym strength.”

You have five distinct kinds of strength: maximal strength, explosive strength, functional strength, aesthetic strength, and aerobic strength. Together, they make up what we call your StrengthSpan: They're the full spectrum of how your body produces and uses force. Because at the end of the day, all strength comes down to force production.

Almost every day, you'll rely on all the components of a complete Strengthspan, using cardio and explosive power to run and catch your train, and relying on functional strength to pick a bag up off the ground. Basketball players, bodybuilders, and powerlifters also use a complete Strengthspan, although they emphasize different portions of it. The new MH Maximum Strengthspan program brings those elements together in one plan, complete with workouts, nutrition guidance, and conditioning work designed to build strength that actually carries over. Train the full spectrum and become stronger in every sense of the word.

Your Strengthspan Prerequisites

Walk 6,000 to 8,000 Steps Per Day

THIS PROGRAM MAY only have you in the gym for about an hour, four to five days per week—but what you do outside the gym still matters. Hitting 6,000 to 8,000 steps per day helps keep your body moving, supports cardiovascular health, and increases your daily energy expenditure, which can help manage body fat over time.

If that number feels high, start with 6,000 steps and gradually work your way up. Small habits add up: take phone calls on your feet, walk between sets at the gym, or use a walking pad while watching TV.

Eat at Maintenance Calories

“In terms of the workout plans we’ve done at Men’s Health, this is going to be one of the least restrictive,” says Samuel. “We want to eat healthy, we want to eat the right things…but you can enjoy life, you can enjoy what you’re eating.”

The goal here is to eat at maintenance, which is enough calories to keep your bodyweight relatively stable while you build strength. This isn’t about aggressive dieting or bulking. It’s about giving your body the fuel it needs to perform, recover, and adapt. If you’re unsure where to start, use a calorie calculator—like this one from the Mayo Clinic—to estimate your maintenance intake, then adjust based on your progress.

Train Hard

“If you’re getting through your eight-to-10-rep sets and you’re not feeling like you’re challenging yourself much, using 25-pound dumbbells for every set, you’re not going to get what you need out of this workout,” says Samuel.

To make this program effective, you need to train with intent. The reps in reserve (RIR) method is a subjective estimation of how many reps you had left in the tank at the end of a set. Your final reps should feel challenging but still controlled. For most sets, aim to finish with about two reps in reserve. If you’re new to training, this can take some practice. A good rule of thumb: if you could have easily done five more reps, the weight is too light. If you couldn’t have done one more with good form, it’s too heavy.

Don’t Forget Your Cardio

That’s right, cardio. You can read more under “Strengthspan training principles” below, but endurance is crucial to sustain the intense workout sessions in this plan. Also, it’s good for you, so, like broccoli, you’ll need to choke down three sessions per week.

“You want to be able to survive on a basketball court, playing with your kids, or if the elevators are out one day at the office and you have to go up the stairs,” explains Samuel. “You want to be prepared for those things… so we want a little bit of a cardio component.”

After three of the four to five prescribed workouts, pick one of the two interval options below for cardio exercise of your choice: burpees, jumping jacks, mountain climbers, air bike, treadmill sprints, whatever you want.

Strengthspan Training Principles

REGARDLESS OF YOUR long-term goals—whether you want a more aesthetic physique or to run a half marathon—building strength should be part of the plan. Strength is your body’s ability to produce force, and it underpins everything you do, from sprinting and jumping to lifting, carrying, and changing direction. The stronger you are, the more force you can produce—and the more efficient and powerful your movement becomes.

But strength isn’t just one thing. A heavy deadlift, an explosive jump, and carrying your kid around all day each demand different types of muscular effort. That’s where the idea of a StrengthSpan comes in—the range of ways your muscles produce force across different speeds, loads, and durations.

Most people train only one part of that spectrum, usually slow, controlled strength. Over time, that leaves gaps in performance and resilience. Training across your full StrengthSpan—building force, expressing it quickly, and sustaining it over time—is what leads to more complete strength, both in the gym and in everyday life.

Absolute Strength

WHAT IT IS: Absolute strength is the highest amount of force you can produce in a single effort, expressed in the gym as the most weight you can lift for one rep. It’s your raw force ceiling. Your top-end capacity. Think of that one-rep max bench press your uncle hit in college and still talks about. That’s absolute strength.

HOW YOU’LL TRAIN IT: During this program, you’ll have two strength-focused days where you’ll work up to a heavy set of four reps. It doesn’t need to be an all-time PR, but it should feel demanding. The goal is to teach your muscles to contract with high levels of force, raising your overall strength ceiling and increasing your long-term power potential.

Explosive Strength

WHAT IT IS: If maximal strength is horsepower, explosive strength is how quickly you apply it. It’s your ability to produce force fast. The rapid, forceful burst required for a wrestler shooting a takedown or a basketball player jumping for a rebound is explosive strength.

HOW YOU’LL TRAIN IT: You can’t move heavy weights quickly, so explosive training relies on lighter loads moved with intent. You’ll have a dedicated power day featuring movements like trap bar jumps and kettlebell cleans, performed for low reps with maximum speed. The weight may be lighter, but the effort should be high. Every rep should feel technical and aggressive.

Functional Strength

WHAT IT IS: Functional strength simply means being strong in the movement patterns you actually use—bending, twisting, lunging, rotating. Real life rarely happens in a straight line and is executed with perfect control. perfectly controlled, straight-line lifts. Carrying three bags of groceries with one arm while holding a toddler in the other is an example of real-world “functional” strength.

HOW YOU’LL TRAIN IT: No, you don’t need to carry your child for max distance. This program trains strength across multiple planes of motion. You’ll perform multi-joint movements like lateral lunges and windmills that challenge stability, coordination, and control. The result is strength that shows up outside the gym.

Aesthetic Strength

WHAT IT IS: Muscle size and strength are closely connected. Bigger muscles have more motor units to produce more force, and getting stronger allows you to handle more weight over time, which is ultimately what fuels more growth. While powerlifters and bodybuilders prioritize different outcomes, both build impressive levels of size and strength.

HOW YOU’LL TRAIN IT: Compound lifts build a strong foundation, but they don’t always fully challenge smaller muscle groups like the arms and shoulders. That’s why this program includes targeted isolation work in the moderate-rep range—typically 8 to 12 reps—to build muscle and support joint health. Progressive overload still applies here: add weight, add reps, and keep tension high.

Aerobic Strength

WHAT IT IS: Aerobic strength is your ability to sustain effort over time and recover between bouts of high output. It supports your heart, lungs, and overall endurance capacity, helping you maintain performance and bounce back between hard sets. Without it, strength work can leave you winded faster than expected.

HOW YOU’LL TRAIN IT: Even in a strength-focused plan, conditioning matters. Dedicated cardio sessions and structured intervals will challenge your aerobic system without interfering with recovery. Samuel has baked some cardio in this plan for exactly that reason.

DAY 1: TOTAL-BODY POWER

Walkout to Scorpion

This mobility drill opens your hips, hamstrings, and thoracic spine while adding a bit of core challenge. It’s a low-intensity way to prepare your body for explosive work by moving through multiple planes of motion and reinforcing control through end ranges. Move deliberately, reaching long through each rep rather than rushing through it.

SETS AND REPS: 3 rounds of 60 seconds on, 30 seconds off

HOW TO DO THE WALKOUT TO SCORPION:

Start standing, then place your hands on the floor. Gradually walk them forward until you’re in pushup position, then lower your chest to the floor.

Keeping your chest plastered to the floor, raise your right leg in the air; try to touch it to the floor near your left hand, stretching your hip and lower back.

Reverse the movement and repeat on the other side, then walk back to standing position.

Trap Bar Deadlift Jump

The point of this exercise is to maximize force production in your lower body. The trap bar lets you load your vertical jump naturally (without using your arms) while keeping you in a strong, athletic position. Don’t go heavy here: “I would honestly think about just throwing even 10- or 25-pound plates onto your trap bar,” instructs Samuel. “The resistance is just giving you a little bit of feedback so you can hit the right position and you're jumping with a little bit more load than you would have from body weight.”

SETS AND REPS: 4 sets of 3 to 5 reps, rest 90 to 120 seconds

HOW TO DO THE TRAP BAR DEADLIFT JUMP:

Position yourself inside the trap bar, with your shins aligned with (or just in front of) the center of the bar.

Push your butt back as far as possible, bend your knees, and reach down to grip the handles. Grip as tightly as possible.

Keep your head in a neutral position, keeping your gazed fixed at something in front of you. Squeeze your shoulder blades to create tension, and turn the pits of your elbows forward, facing out.

Make sure your hips are lower than your shoulders, then prepare to initiate the lift.

Dumbbell Reverse Lunge with Knee Drive

This single-leg movement builds strength while reinforcing balance and coordination. The reverse lunge strengthens the quad muscles, while the knee drive adds a dynamic motion, “that's going to very much simulate a little bit of what happens when we go into any running stride or when we're jumping on a basketball court,” Samuel says. Stay controlled on the way down and drive up with intent.

SETS AND REPS: 3 sets of 6 to 8 reps per leg, rest 60 to 90 seconds

HOW TO DO THE DUMBBELL REVERSE LUNGE WITH KNEE DRIVE:

Stand with your feet shoulder-width apart holding dumbbells in each hand at hip-level. Squeeze your shoulder blades, abs, and glutes, keeping your gaze neutral at a point straight ahead of you.

Step one leg back and slightly out, landing with your toe first. Work to avoid slamming your knee into the ground. Keep your chest in an upright position, bending your knees to form right angles with both of your legs.

Push off the ground with your front foot and drive your rear knee up powerfully. Keep your torso in a solid upright position by squeezing your core to stay balanced.

Step directly back into another lunge rep.

Push Press + Chinup

This upper-body superset pairs an explosive overhead press with a strict vertical pull. The push press trains your legs to drive the weight overhead, teaching you to transfer force from your lower body to your upper extremities. Chin-ups are just a classic way to build strength, muscle, and stability in your torso. Pairing them together makes sense since they cover your vertical pushing and pulling patterns.

SETS AND REPS: 3 sets of 8 reps for the push press and chinups to technical failure, rest 90 seconds

HOW TO DO THE PUSH PRESS:

Start in a standard military press position, holding a barbell or pair of dumbbells in both hands at shoulder-level.

Dip your hips and knees slightly and explode upwards, rising onto your toes.

Use that upward energy to help propel the weight overhead. Make sure you keep your abs and glutes tight.

Lower the weight back to the starting position under control.

HOW TO DO THE CHINUP:

Get into a good hanging position using an underhand grip, squeezing your shoulders, abs, and glutes to maintain tension.

Drive your elbows into your hips to pull yourself up to the bar. Continue until your head is above the bar, pausing briefly at the top.

Lower back down until your elbows are extended, maintaining control of the position and keeping tension.

Kettlebell Dead Clean

“You're going to start from a very good kettlebell deadlift position, and then you're…going to stand explosively, getting onto your toes and hitting triple extension, and then you’ll pull the kettlebell up to your chest,” explains Samuel. By having you start each rep from a “dead” stop, this kettlebell clean variation builds explosivity in your hip extension while reinforcing proper form mechanics. Pulling from the floor helps you generate power from a full stop, making each rep more demanding.

SETS AND REPS: 3 sets of 6 to 8 reps per side, rest 60 to 90 seconds

HOW TO DO THE KETTLEBELL DEAD CLEAN:

Start standing slightly behind your kettlebell, feet slightly wider than shoulder width core tight.

Hinge forward at the waist and push your butt back, lowering your torso and grasping the kettlebell firmly with both hands.

Turn the pits of your elbows forward, powering up your lats and midback muscles. Maintain this tension.

Tighten your glutes and hamstrings, driving your butt a few inches lower. Maintain this tension.

Stand explosively, pulling the kettlebell up to your chest, shifting your hands slightly to hold the weight by the horns (sides of the handle).

Lower the weight back to the floor under control.

DAY 2: UPPER BODY STRENGTH/HYPERTROPHY

Two-Way Open Book

This mobility drill targets your thoracic spine and shoulders, helping you move more freely and comfortably through pressing and pulling patterns. Rotating through both directions improves range of motion and can reduce stiffness that limits upper-body training.

SETS AND REPS: 2 rounds of 60 seconds per side, rest 30 seconds

HOW TO DO THE TWO-WAY OPEN BOOK:

Start by lying on the ground, placing your hips and knees at about a 90-degree angle. Squeeze a yoga block (or similar item) between your knees for support. The additional squeeze will help fire up your lower spine and hips, allowing you to get a stronger thoracic twist from your chest and shoulder area.

Squeeze your knees and drive your hip into the ground, then begin reaching your arm as far behind you as possible—again, as if you’re opening up the pages of a book.

Twist with the intention of touching your wrist to the ground behind you (although you may not reach that distance at first). However, go as far as possible as your chest continues opening and stretching before returning to start.

Go for another rep, again squeezing your chest and shoulders throughout the stretch.

Dumbbell Dead Stop Row

The only difference between a dead stop row and a traditional dumbbell row is that you’ll rest the dumbbell on the floor for a moment between each rep to eliminate momentum. “And because you get to rest to pull that dumbbell off the ground, you get to be a little bit more explosive,” Samuel says. “So even though this isn't a power day, we do get a little bit of a power component in there”. This forces your back muscles to generate maximal force on every repetition, allows you to use more weight, and builds core strength as you resist torso rotation on every pull.

SETS AND REPS: 4 sets of 6 to 8 reps, rest 90 seconds

HOW TO DO THE DUMBBELL DEAD STOP ROW:

Stand with your feet at shoulder width apart in front of the bench, with a heavy dumbbell on the floor. Push your butt back and lower your torso down, extending your off arm to rest your palm on the bench. Make sure your shoulders stay above your hips.

Grab the dumbbell with your working hand. Squeeze your glutes and abs to create full-body tension. Your back should be flat, with your head in a neutral position.

Squeeze your mid-back muscles to drive your elbow up, rowing the weight. Keep your shoulders level and avoid rotating your lower back.

Pause for a beat, then lower the weight back down to the floor with control.

Incline Dumbbell Press

The incline press targets your upper chest and shoulders, allowing you to get a deeper stretch than a barbell does. It’s a staple for building upper-body size and strength, especially when performed with control. “On the last set, I want you to go heavy enough that you're pushing for six reps, but it’s fine if you fall a little bit short on that,” says Samuel. This exercise is going to cover us for our heavy push in our workout.”

SETS AND REPS: 3 sets of 6 to 10 reps, rest 90 seconds

HOW TO DO THE INCLINE DUMBBELL PRESS:

Sit on the incline bench set to a 45 to 60 degree angle holding a set of dumbbells. Drive your feet into the floor, drive your glutes into the seat, squeeze your abs, and drive your shoulders into the back pad.

Raise the dumbbells to your shoulders. If the weight is too heavy, use your knees to kick the bell up. Your forearms should be perpendicular to the floor.

Press the weight straight up, keeping your upper arms perpendicular to the ground.

Once the weight is up, drive your shoulders down to create tension. Shift the angle of your arms by turning your hands slightly in.

Lower the weight back down toward your chest as your chest flexibility allows, at least until your elbows are below your shoulders.

Cable Fly + Pushup

The goal of this superset is absolute muscular fatigue. The cable or dumbbell fly pre-exhausts the muscles with controlled tension, while pushups extend the set and drive additional volume. Use lighter weights on the fly and take the pushups close to failure.

SETS AND REPS: 3 sets of 8 to 10 reps for the fly, then 25 pushups using rest-pause as needed, rest 60 to 90 seconds

HOW TO DO THE CABLE FLY:

Attach two stirrup handles to the high-pulley cables of a cable-crossover station. Grab a handle with each hand, and stand in a staggered stance in the middle of the station.

Your arms should be outstretched but slightly bent. Lean forward slightly at your hips; don’t round your back.

Without changing the bend in your arms, bring your elbows together. Scoop your pinkies up, squeezing your chest. Pause and hold the contraction.

Slowly reverse the movement.

HOW TO DO THE PUSHUP:

Start in a high plank position, with your palms flat on the floor, stacked directly below your shoulders.

Squeeze your shoulders, glutes, and core to create full-body tension. Your spine should form a straight line, with a neutral spine.

Bend your elbows to descend to the floor, stopping with your chest just above the ground. Your elbows should be at a 45 degree angle relative to the torso.

Press back up off the floor, raising up to the top position with your elbows fully extended.

Dumbbell Pullover

The dumbbell pullover bridges your chest and lats, helping you build upper-body size while improving shoulder mobility. It’s especially useful for adding volume without overly fatiguing pressing muscles. “This is going to help stretch you out,” says Samuel. “It's going to help open up your thoracic spine; it’s going to help you learn to arch your back, and that's all really important stuff from a mobility standpoint in the long term.”

SETS AND REPS: 2 sets of 8 reps, rest 60 seconds

HOW TO DO THE DUMBBELL PULLOVER:

Half-Full Goblet Squat

This is your typical squat with an extra half-rep thrown in for every rep: You’ll squat down, come up halfway, squat back down, and then stand all the way up. Doing so increases the tension on your quads and glutes compared to the same number of full-rep squats. “You want this to feel really, really hard,” says Samuel. “Make sure that you're going heavy and you're really trying to get depth on these squats.”

SETS AND REPS: 3 sets of 10 to 12 reps, rest 60 to 90 seconds

HOW TO DO THE HALF-FULL GOBLET SQUAT:

Push up off the floor, standing until you hit the halfway point. Pause, then lower back down to the bottom position.

Hip 90/90 with Reaches

This drill improves hip mobility and rotational control, both of which are essential for athletic movement. Adding reaches challenges your core and increases the range you can access. Move slowly and maintain control throughout each transition.

HOW TO DO THE HIP 90/90 WITH REACHES:

Sit on the ground with your legs extended in front of you, keeping them bent at 90 degrees and your weight on your heels.

Keeping your legs bent at 90 degrees, rotate your legs to the left until the outside of your thighs are pressed against the ground. Reach in the direction you rotated to emphasize the stretch.

Peel your legs off the floor, pivoting on your heels and rotating your thighs up and over to the other side until your legs are again pressed into the floor.

Fixed-Position Lateral Lunge + Staggered-Stance Kettlebell Swing

This is a strong pairing for mobility and power development. The lateral lunge gets you moving side-to-side, which you probably don’t do as often as you should, and works your inside thigh muscles (adductors). The staggered stance swing builds explosiveness in the hips.

SETS AND REPS: 3 sets of 30 seconds per exercise, rest 30 to 60 seconds

HOW TO DO THE FIXED-POSITION LATERAL LUNGE:

Start standing, holding a kettlebell or dumbbell at your chest in a goblet position, core tight.

Step to the right a few feet with your right leg, taking a relatively large step.

Land. Keeping your left leg straight, bend your right knee and push your butt back, lowering slowly. "Your torso may lean forward slightly," says Samuel. "That's OK and natural."

Lower as far as you can comfortably, aiming to get thigh parallel to the ground. Then explosively drive up and to the left, driving back to a standing position.

HOW TO DO THE STAGGERED-STANCE KETTLEBELL SWING:

Begin in a standard swing starting position a few feet away from the bell—but step one foot back slightly, keeping your hips and shoulders square.

Push you butt back, then lean over to grip the bell's handle. Hold on tightly.

Squeeze your shoulder blades, then shift your arms to turn the pits of your elbows outward. Squeeze your abs to create tension. Keep your eyes on the floor to maintain a neutral spine.

"Hike" the weight back between your legs, keeping your knees slightly bent. Keep your arms straight.

Stand straight up, locking your knees, and aggressively squeeze your glutes to perform the swing. Keep your arms loose, like ropes connecting yourself to the kettlebell. Don't aim for a certain point; allow the momentum to determine how high the bell goes.

Allow the weight to fall back down, back through your legs.

Hanging Leg Raise

This core exercise targets your abs through controlled hip flexion and pelvic movement. It builds strength and stability in the anterior core while also challenging your grip and shoulder control. Focus on smooth, controlled reps rather than swinging.

SETS AND REPS: 3 sets of 8 reps, rest 60 seconds

HOW TO DO THE HANGING LEG RAISE:

Start by grasping the bar tightly, with your palms over the bar instead of holding on with your fingers. Don't jump up and adjust your hands; if you need, step up from a box to make sure you get a good grip.

Lift your legs off the floor and pull your shoulders down, squeezing to engage your upper back. Turn the pits of your elbows forward to activate your lats. Squeeze your abs and glutes. Your legs should be slightly in front of your torso.

Bend your knees slightly, then curl your legs up. Think about showing your butt to someone standing in front of you.

Lower back down to the starting position.

HOW TO DO THE HAMMER CURL:

Stand with your feet hip-width apart, holding a pair of dumbbells in a neutral grip (palms facing each other). Squeeze your shoulder blades, abs, and glutes to create full-body tension.

Curl the dumbbell up, moving only at the elbow joint. Keep your upper arms still and perpendicular to the floor. Squeeze your biceps at the top of the movement.

Avoid shifting your elbow forward to keep your shoulders out of the movement.

Lower the weight back down to the starting position under control.

HOW TO DO THE TRICEPS PRESSDOWN:

Stand holding the cable rope at chest height with a neutral grip, with your shoulders, abs, and glutes engaged to create tension. Your elbows should be tight to either side of your torso.

Press down, moving only at the elbows. Squeeze your triceps at the bottom of the cable's range.

To avoid cheating, support your back on an incline bench and perform the movement.
