# Why Are My Eyes Bloodshot? Don’t Ignore These Urgent Signs | Ubie Doctor's Note

Why are my eyes bloodshot?

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Bloodshot eyes have many possible causes. Common, minor triggers include dryness, allergies, irritants, eye strain, contact lens problems, or a small subconjunctival hemorrhage. More serious causes include eye infections (like conjunctivitis), uveitis, corneal ulcers, and acute angle-closure glaucoma. In some cases, red eyes may signal systemic conditions such as high blood pressure or autoimmune disease.

Key red flags that require urgent medical care include severe eye pain, vision changes, light sensitivity, thick discharge, or recent eye injury. For milder cases, home remedies like artificial tears, cool compresses, and avoiding irritants often help.

Why Are My Eyes Bloodshot?

Bloodshot eyes—also called ocular redness—are a common concern. They occur when tiny blood vessels on the white part of your eye (the sclera) become enlarged or irritated. While often harmless, redness can sometimes signal serious conditions. Below is a complete overview of possible causes, simple relief measures, warning signs, and when to seek medical care.

Common Causes of Bloodshot Eyes

Dryness and Irritation• Prolonged screen time, low-humidity environments or wind exposure• Inadequate blinking or contact lens overwear• Symptoms: gritty feeling, mild burning

Allergies• Pollen, pet dander, dust mites or certain cosmetics• Histamine release leads to itching, tearing and redness• Often seasonal; may accompany sneezing or nasal congestion

Infection (Conjunctivitis)• Viral conjunctivitis ("pink eye")• Bacterial conjunctivitis—thick discharge, eyelids stuck together• Azari & Barney (2013) systematic review: infection is a top cause of eye redness

Subconjunctival Hemorrhage• Tiny blood vessel breaks, causing a bright red patch• Triggers: forceful coughing, sneezing, heavy lifting or vomiting• Usually harmless; resolves in 1–2 weeks without treatment

Eye Strain• Focusing on near tasks (reading, screens) for hours• Leads to muscle fatigue, redness and headache in some people

Environmental Irritants• Smoke, chlorine (pools), chemical fumes• Symptoms improve once irritant is removed

Systemic Health Issues• High blood pressure• Bleeding disorders or blood‐thinning medications• Liver disease (e.g., cirrhosis) can reduce clotting factors, raising risk of subconjunctival bleeds (D'Amico et al., 2006; Parkes et al., 2010)• Autoimmune conditions: rheumatoid arthritis, lupus

Less Common but Serious Causes

Acute Glaucoma• Sudden eye pain, blurred vision, halos around lights• May be accompanied by nausea

Uveitis (Inflammation Inside the Eye)• Deep, aching pain, light sensitivity• Requires prompt treatment to prevent vision loss

Corneal Ulcer or Abrasion• Severe pain, tearing, "foreign body" sensation• Risk of infection and scarring if untreated

Scleritis or Episcleritis• Inflammation of scleral tissues; may signal autoimmune disease• Deep, boring pain versus superficial irritation

Home Care and Relief Strategies

Most minor cases of bloodshot eyes improve with simple measures:

Cool Compress• Apply a clean, damp cloth over closed eyelids for 5–10 minutes• Reduces swelling and soothes irritation

Lubricating Eye Drops ("Artificial Tears")• Use preservative-free drops 3–4 times daily for dryness or minor redness• Avoid vasoconstrictor ("red-out") drops—they can worsen redness if overused

Limit Screen Time and Take Breaks• Follow the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look 20 feet away for 20 seconds• Blink consciously to keep eyes moist

Avoid Rubbing Your Eyes• Rubbing can damage surface tissue and introduce bacteria• If itchy, use cool compress or antihistamine drops instead

Maintain Proper Contact Lens Hygiene• Replace lenses and cases as recommended• Never sleep in lenses unless specifically approved

Manage Allergies• Keep windows closed during high pollen seasons• Wash bedding frequently; consider air purifiers

Preventive Tips

Wear protective eyewear in windy, dusty or chemical environments

Stay hydrated—good overall hydration supports tear production

Ensure adequate sleep; fatigue can worsen redness

If you smoke, consider quitting; tobacco smoke is a known irritant

Monitor and control systemic conditions (e.g., hypertension, diabetes)

Understanding Underlying Conditions

While most bloodshot eyes are benign, they may sometimes hint at underlying health issues:

Liver Disease and Bleeding RiskChronic liver conditions—such as cirrhosis—can impair production of clotting factors. This increases the likelihood of subconjunctival hemorrhages (D'Amico et al., 2006).

Autoimmune DisordersConditions like rheumatoid arthritis or lupus can cause ocular inflammation requiring systemic treatment.

Infectious DiseasesBeyond conjunctivitis, infections such as herpes simplex or zoster can involve the eye surface or deeper tissues. Early antiviral therapy is critical.

Next Steps and When to Talk to a Doctor

If your bloodshot eyes do not improve with home care, or if you notice any red flags, make an appointment with an eye care professional or your primary doctor. Prompt evaluation ensures identification of:

Infections needing antibiotics or antivirals

Inflammations requiring steroids or immunosuppressants

Glaucoma or uveitis that could threaten vision

Systemic diseases impacting eye health
