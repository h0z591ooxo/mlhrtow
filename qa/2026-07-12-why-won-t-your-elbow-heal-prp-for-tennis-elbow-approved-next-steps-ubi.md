Why Won't Your Elbow Heal? PRP for Tennis Elbow & Approved Next Steps

Elbow pain is common in athletes, hobbyists and anyone whose daily tasks involve repetitive wrist or arm motions. When simple rest and ice don't resolve the ache, frustration sets in. Understanding why your elbow won't heal—and what to do about it—can help you regain function and get back to the activities you love.

Why Your Elbow May Refuse to Heal

Tendon Degeneration, Not Just Inflammation

Tennis elbow (lateral epicondylitis) involves microtears and collagen breakdown in the tendon attaching forearm muscles to the elbow.

Over time, poor blood flow and chronic micro-injury prevent proper healing.

Repetitive Strain and Poor Mechanics

Tasks like tennis, painting, typing or manual labor can overload the same tendon fibers day after day.

Improper technique or equipment (e.g., grips that are too large or small) magnify stress on the tendon.

Insufficient Rest and Rehabilitation

Simply avoiding pain-causing activities isn't enough. Tendons need graduated loading through specific exercises to rebuild strength and flexibility.

Skipping or doing the wrong exercises can stall recovery.

Underlying Health Factors

Diabetes, smoking and poor circulation can slow tissue repair.

Age-related tendon changes may also diminish the body's natural healing capacity.

Misdiagnosis or Concomitant Issues

Other elbow conditions (nerve entrapment, arthritis or biceps tendon problems) can mimic or accompany tennis elbow, confusing the treatment plan.

A thorough exam or imaging study may be needed if pain persists beyond 6–12 weeks.

What Is PRP and How It Works

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is an injectable concentration of your own platelets suspended in a small volume of plasma. Platelets contain growth factors that can kick-start the body's natural repair processes.

Preparation: A small blood sample is drawn, then spun in a centrifuge to separate platelets.

Injection: Under ultrasound guidance, the PRP is delivered precisely into the damaged tendon area.

Healing Cascade: Growth factors released by platelets stimulate new collagen formation, increase blood vessel growth and modulate inflammation.

When conservative measures (rest, bracing, physical therapy) fall short, many clinicians turn to PRP for tennis elbow as a safe, minimally invasive option.

Evidence for PRP in Tennis Elbow

Multiple clinical studies have compared PRP for tennis elbow against steroid injections, saline placebo and shockwave therapy:

Longer-Lasting Relief: While steroids may offer quick pain relief, PRP often leads to more durable improvements in pain and function at 6- and 12-month follow-up.

Tendon Remodeling: Imaging studies show improved tendon structure months after PRP injections—something not seen with steroids.

Safety Profile: Because PRP uses your own blood, allergic reactions are rare and there's no risk of tendon rupture associated with repeated steroid shots.

Although protocols vary (single vs. multiple injections, different platelet concentrations), the bulk of current evidence supports considering PRP for tennis elbow when standard therapies haven't done the job.

Who Is a Good Candidate?

You may be a candidate for PRP if:

You have a confirmed diagnosis of chronic tennis elbow (symptoms > 3–6 months).

You've tried at least 6–12 weeks of conservative care (rest or activity modification, elbow braces, NSAIDs, physical therapy with eccentric exercises).

You prefer to avoid—or have not responded to—corticosteroid injections.

You understand that full benefit can take 2–3 months and that some patients need two or three injections spaced 4–6 weeks apart.

PRP is generally not recommended if you have an active infection, malignancy or certain blood disorders. Always discuss your full medical history and medications with your provider.

Risks and Considerations

PRP is low-risk, but it's important to know:

Mild pain or bruising at the injection site for 1–3 days.

Rare infection risk—practitioners use sterile technique to minimize this.

Not a guaranteed cure: about 70–80% of patients report meaningful improvement; a minority may require additional injections or surgical referral.
