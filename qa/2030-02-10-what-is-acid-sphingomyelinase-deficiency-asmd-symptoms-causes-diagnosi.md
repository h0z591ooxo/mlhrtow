# What Is Acid Sphingomyelinase Deficiency (ASMD)? Symptoms, Causes, Diagnosis, Treatment, and Prevention

What Is Acid Sphingomyelinase Deficiency (ASMD)? Symptoms, Causes, Diagnosis, Treatment, and Prevention

Acid sphingomyelinase deficiency (ASMD) is a rare, progressive, inherited genetic disorder that’s caused by a deficiency in an enzyme called acid sphingomyelinase.

This enzyme is needed to break down (metabolize) a fatty substance (lipid) called sphingomyelin, which has significant structural and functional roles in the cell. It’s a major component of cell membranes, including the myelin sheath that surrounds and electrically insulates nerve cells, and also plays important roles in cellular transport, communication, and apoptosis, or programmed cell death.

As sphingomyelin and other lipids accumulate in various tissues of the body, they can cause damage to several key organs, including the liver, spleen, lungs, and central nervous system (CNS), or the brain and spine.

The symptoms of ASMD can first appear during infancy or later in childhood, and its severity can vary dramatically from one person to another, sometimes even among members of the same family.

Lysosomal Storage Diseases

ASMD is a subtype of Niemann-Pick disease, a group of inherited disorders in which lipids build up in the brain, spleen, liver, lungs, and bone marrow because of defective or insufficient amounts of the enzymes that normally break down those substances.

Niemann-Pick disease is, in turn, one of a group of diseases known as lysosomal storage diseases.

Normally, tiny bodies within cells called lysosomes metabolize lipids, proteins, and carbohydrates into smaller components to provide energy for the body. In lysosomal storage diseases, fats, proteins, and sugars that can’t be broken down correctly, usually because of missing or defective enzymes, are stored in lysosomes, resulting in damage to the organs in which these materials accumulate.

While each individual lysosomal storage disease is rare, collectively, they are common, according to StatPearls.

Niemann-Pick Disease

Acid sphingomyelinase deficiency refers to Niemann-Pick types A and B, which are associated with the SMPD1 gene. There are also Niemann-Pick types C1 and C2, which are caused by mutations in the NPC1 and NPC2 genes.

Symptoms of Niemann-Pick disease may include ataxia (lack of muscle control during voluntary movements such as walking), loss of muscle tone, brain degeneration, increased sensitivity to touch, spasticity (stiff muscles and awkward movement), and slurred speech, according to the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NINDS). Other symptoms may include feeding and swallowing difficulties, eye paralysis, learning problems, and an enlarged liver and spleen. There may be clouding of the cornea, and a characteristic cherry-red halo develops around the center of the retina.

Acid Sphingomyelinase Deficiency (ASMD)

ASMD has traditionally been broken down into two subgroups: neuronopathic (type A) and non-neuronopathic (type B).

Type A, the more severe form, occurs most often in Jewish families and involves damage to brain cells (neurons); it generally causes severe neurodegenerative symptoms during infancy. Additional symptoms include weakness, an enlarged liver and spleen, and swollen lymph nodes. It has a grim prognosis and no known cure. Children with this type of ASMD rarely live beyond 18 months.

Type B ASMD, called juvenile onset, isn’t considered a neurologic disorder and often has milder symptoms that include lack of muscle control and peripheral neuropathy (nerve damage). The brain is generally not affected. Other symptoms include an enlarged liver and spleen and lung problems.

Signs and Symptoms of Acid Sphingomyelinase Deficiency

ASMD is a highly variable disorder, meaning it affects people who have it differently, depending on what form they have.

As a result, every individual case is unique. Some children will develop severe, life-threatening symptoms and related health complications during infancy, while others have mild symptoms and may go undiagnosed well into adulthood.

Parents should talk to their child’s care team about specific symptoms and complications and overall prognosis.

Niemann-Pick Disease Type A

Children who develop Niemann-Pick disease type A ASMD during infancy will initially experience an enlarged liver or spleen (a condition called hepatosplenomegaly). This organ enlargement often worsens, causing accumulation of fluid in the abdomen (ascites) as well as yellowing of the skin and whites of the eyes (jaundice).

Other symptoms of Niemann-Pick disease type A ASMD include:

Feeding problems

Constipation

Nausea and vomiting

Severe gastrointestinal reflux (GERD)

Irritability

Compromised reflexes and progressive loss of muscle tone (hypotonia)

Feeding difficulties and frequent nausea and vomiting can cause poor nutrition and, thus, failure to reach certain growth milestones.

In addition, sphingomyelin accumulation in the lungs can lead to frequent, severe respiratory infections and difficulty breathing, potentially resulting in life-threatening lung failure.

Most infants with Niemann-Pick disease type A ASMD will also develop cherry red spots in their eyes. This condition affects the macula, or the area of the retina that contains the light-sensing cells necessary for central vision.

In some cases of infant-onset Niemann-Pick disease type A ASMD, attainment of developmental milestones and development may be normal during the first several months after birth. However, by 9 to 12 months, those children may see their development plateau and lose already acquired motor skills.

These infants may also experience significant declines in brain function as well as increased muscle tone stiffness (spasticity).

Niemann-Pick Disease Type B

Meanwhile, those with later-onset forms of ASMD — collectively referred to as Niemann-Pick disease type B — can develop symptoms from infancy well into adulthood.

Their symptoms are also generally milder than those seen in people with infant-onset Niemann-Pick disease type A ASMD.

As with infant-onset type A, hepatosplenomegaly, or abnormal enlargement of the liver or spleen, is a common initial symptom.

Progressive enlargement of the spleen can cause low counts of blood platelets (thrombocytopenia), which can lead to bleeding disorders, and white blood cells, which can hurt the body’s ability to fight infections.

In addition, an enlarged spleen may rupture, potentially resulting in life-threatening bleeding into the abdominal cavity.

Some people with Niemann-Pick disease type B experience mild to severe liver disease, including abnormal liver blood tests and scarring in the liver. The liver scarring can range from mild, without symptoms, to cirrhosis and liver failure.

People with late-onset type B ASMD will also see a gradual decline in their lung function and may eventually have difficulty breathing during physical activity (a condition called dyspnea). As a result, many with this form of the disorder will become dependent on supplemental oxygen or mechanical ventilation to maintain breathing.

They may also be susceptible to chronic or recurrent pneumonia.

This places them at high risk for early coronary artery disease, a common form of heart disease.

All forms of ASMD are caused by a deficiency of an enzyme called acid sphingomyelinase, which is needed to break down (metabolize) a fatty substance (lipid) called sphingomyelin.

Accumulated sphingomyelin can damage several key organs, including the brain and spine, leading to ASMD symptoms.

Sphingomyelin deficiency occurs as a result of mutations in the sphingomyelin phophodiesterase-1 (SMPD1) gene, which provides the body with instructions for creating the enzyme.

Mutations in the SMPD1 gene are inherited in an autosomal recessive manner, meaning the disease occurs when a child inherits a non-working gene from each parent.

If a person receives one normal copy of a gene and one with a mutation, the person will be a carrier for the ASMD, but may not have symptoms.

If both parents are carriers of an SMPD1 gene mutation, their risk for having a child with ASMD is 25 percent with each pregnancy.

Their risk for having a child who is a carrier is 50 percent with each pregnancy.

The chance for a child to receive normal copies of the SMPD1 gene from both parents and not develop ASMD or be a carrier for the disease is 25 percent.

The risk is the same for males and females.

How Is Acid Sphingomyelinase Deficiency Diagnosed?

ASMD is typically diagnosed based on the presence of its key symptoms during a physician examination and detailed patient history, although some states test for it in their newborn screening panel, per Baby's First Test.

Symptoms can also be confirmed by testing blood to measure for ASM levels.

Even in the absence of symptoms, the siblings of a child with ASMD should be tested to see whether they also have the disorder, according to GeneReviews.

Genetic testing can confirm a diagnosis of ASMD by detecting mutations in the SMPD1 gene known to cause the disorder, but such tests aren’t widely available and may not be covered by private insurance.

Prognosis of Acid Sphingomyelinase Deficiency

Because of the broad spectrum of the severity of ASMD, the lifespan of people who have it is highly variable, ranging from a very early death to a long life with severe health complications.

Niemann-Pick disease type A is often fatal by age 3.

However, people with mild Niemann-Pick disease type B can live until late adulthood, and some may go undiagnosed until well into adulthood.

Duration of Acid Sphingomyelinase Deficiency

Because there’s no known cure for any form of ASMD, a person with the condition, once diagnosed, will have it for their entire lifetime.

However, how long that is depends on the type of ASMD they have.

Treatment and Medication Options for Acid Sphingomyelinase Deficiency

Children and adults with ASMD may require care from a multidisciplinary team of specialists that includes pediatricians (for children), neurologists, hepatologists, cardiologists, and ophthalmologists, among others.

In addition, psychosocial support for those with the condition and their families is essential.

Those with ASMD and their families may also benefit from genetic counseling, which may help prospective parents assess their risk for passing it on to their children.

As there’s no cure for the condition, most treatment focuses on managing its symptoms.

For example, for children with Niemann-Pick type A ASMD, physical and occupational therapy may help maintain balance and muscle strength.

Young people with the condition may need regular nutritional assessments to ensure they’re eating enough and maintaining a healthy weight. Some children may be fitted with a feeding (gastronomy) tube to maintain proper nutrition.

In this procedure, a thin tube is inserted into the stomach through a small incision in the abdomen. The tube enables direct intake of food and medicine into the stomach and digestive tract.

Nutritional support may also be needed in people with Niemann-Pick type B ASMD to ensure they receive sufficient high-quality calories as well as proper calcium and vitamin D intake, the latter to reduce their risk for low bone density (osteopenia).

Proper diet may also help reduce their risk for high blood fat counts (dyslipidemia).

Those who have sleep problems due to Niemann-Pick type A or type B ASMD can be treated with sedative medications.

Treatment for dyslipidemia may be required in some adults with type B disease.

People with Niemann-Pick type B who experience prolonged bleeding due to low platelet counts (thrombocytopenia) may need regular blood transfusions.

Similarly, those who have lung or breathing problems caused by ASMD may require supplemental oxygen to maintain breathing.

Adults with high cholesterol caused by ASMD may need cholesterol-lowering medication to maintain healthy levels. In addition, they may need to regularly consult with a cardiologist as they may be at higher risk for heart disease.

Finally, some adults with liver failure due to Niemann-Pick type B ASMD may need a liver transplant.

Medication Options for Acid Sphingomyelinase Deficiency

The U.S Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved a prescription medication called olipudase alfa (Xenpozyme) to treat pediatric and adult patients with ASMD in 2022.

It’s the first approved medication to treat symptoms that aren’t related to the central nervous system in people with ASMD.

The drug is designed to be an enzyme replacement therapy (ERT). The goal of treatment is to replace the missing enzyme, acid sphingomyelinase, with a genetically engineered form that helps reduce sphingomyelin accumulation in the liver, spleen, and lungs.

Researchers are also evaluating gene therapy for people with lysosomal storage diseases like ASMD. In gene therapy, the defective gene is replaced with a normal gene to help boost production of the acid sphingomyelinase enzyme and prevent the development of ASMD.

Although this could ultimately provide a cure for the condition, research is still in the early stages.

Alternative and Complementary Therapies

There are no proven alternative and complementary therapies that help with the symptoms of ASMD. However, your care team may recommend certain nutritional supplements to ensure your body has adequate levels of vital nutrients such as calcium and vitamin D, particularly in infants with the condition who experience feeding problems as a result.

Complications of Acid Sphingomyelinase Deficiency

Infants with Niemann-Pick type A typically develop an enlarged liver or spleen or both within the first few months of life. As a result, they often fail to grow at an average rate.

As their condition progresses, they may also experience:

Loss of motor and intellectual skills

Lung damage

Frequent lung infections

Life-threatening lung failure

Heart problems due to elevated LDL levels

Older children and adults with Niemann-Pick type B may experience long-term health complications, including:

Enlarged liver or spleen

Frequent and severe lung infections

Abnormally low blood platelet counts

Poor bone development, leading to a higher risk for fractures

Slowed growth

Many people with ASMD suffer from depression and sometimes exhibit psychotic symptoms consistent with schizophrenia.

The condition has also been associated with an increased risk of Parkinson’s disease.

Research and Statistics: Who Has Acid Sphingomyelinase Deficiency?

It’s estimated that ASMD occurs in one in 250,000 people in the general population, though some cases go mis- or undiagnosed, per the National Organization for Rare Disorders.

Niemann-Pick type A affects all racial and ethnic groups, but occurs more frequently in those of Ashkenazi Jewish descent.

Niemann-Pick type B affects all racial and ethnic groups equally.
