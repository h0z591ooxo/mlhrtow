# An Anxiety-Free Guide to Keeping Up With the News

TODAY, WHEN I feel despair over how long it is taking me to correct for lockdown’s effects on my attention span and general cognition, it helps to remember how much worse I felt during the early days of the pandemic. My average screen time hit six hours per day. At some point in 2020, I lost the ability to read anything but the airport-iest of airport books. I watched all of Queen’s Gambit in one weekend but retained nothing. I spent so much time laying on my stomach, propping myself up on my elbows so I could look at my phone, that I injured my neck. Most of these behaviors were tempered by the return to post-lockdown life. (It does not soothe me that the average American screen time is over seven hours; a six-hour screen time is incompatible with a productive and pleasant life.) But one has lingered.

Shortly before the pandemic arrived, I began commuting to a new job by car. Previously, I’d ridden the train an hour to and from a co-working space, and had spent the time in transit reading magazines. I would read each magazine completely, and when I had exhausted its content and destroyed its pages pulling it in and out of my tote, I would recycle it with satisfaction. With that transit change, my subscriptions piled up unread—a bad look for a magazine writer. I tried to read on my phone during my lunches, but if a text arrived, or if I remembered that I needed to send an email, I inevitably wound up on Instagram.

Lockdown further devastated my focus. I would read the opening of an article, but then a bolt of anxiety would hit, causing me to drift to my phone to see if there was any pandemic news to read. I did not understand how long it takes to develop a vaccine, and I checked Twitter constantly in those early months of 2020 just in case one had been announced. Then the presidential election was upon us, and it felt hugely consequential and suspenseful. I suppose it did, at least: I honestly have few concrete recollections of that time. I do know that that was around the time I became a news rat, seeking dopamine by refreshing my feeds over and over, but receiving only more stress and dread and suspense. I was seeing more news than I’d ever seen before, but processing very little.

In summer 2022, a friend read a book called Stolen Focus: Why You Can’t Pay Attention—and How to Think Deeply Again, an international bestseller by Johann Hari. I ordered it immediately on her recommendation but did not actually pick it up until summer of 2023, because my focus had been stolen. But when I did, I was fascinated. Through interviews, studies, and an experiment in which he decamped to Provincetown for an internet-free three months, Hari explores the mechanisms by which our phones and apps are keeping us from accessing states of deep focus. I was particularly struck by on one chapter in which Hari investigates why, when he was reading three newspapers every morning in Provincetown, he felt less panicked than when he was taking in news via texts from friends, or via live updates on social media. I paused reading, pulled out my phone (bad), and set up a weekend print subscription to the New York Times (good).

Hari referred to the “constant drip-feed of anxiety-provoking factoids” that comes our way on social media. I felt this acutely: where I used to have big, hearty news meals a couple times a day, now I was constantly grazing, taking in a news almond here and a news Pringle there, but never feeling particularly satisfied. Hari invoked a Danish physicist—Sune Lehmann—whose research has recently leaned sociological.

In 2019, Lehmann and three other researchers created a model showing how the amount of information produced and consumed each day has accelerated massively, exhausting our attention resources. When I asked Lehmann how that acceleration leads to stress, he replied that to process all the information available to us today, we have to become “managers” of our attention: “You have to make rules and systems to get through the day, and be intentional about your attention. That in itself is taxing. It’s not what we were designed for, I think.” He noted that ahead of the 2016 U.S. election, he had had a particularly difficult time managing his attention, because the outcome felt so important.

I became a NEWS RAT, seeking DOPAMINE by refreshing my feeds over and over, but receiving only MORE STRESS and DREAD and SUSPENSE.

Once I began receiving the paper-paper again after a multi-decade hiatus (my parents received a newspaper daily when I was growing up), I noticed that on days when I read the paper over breakfast—Friday through Sunday—my screen time was much lower throughout the rest of the day, my focus was much deeper, and my anxiety was minimal. I was also reading more expansively than I do when I rely on an algorithm to curate my news. I would read the front-page stories, but then I would flip to the international section and read about an event that never would have come my way online. It was soothing and diverting to balance out articles about the most salient dramas of the day with less urgent news. Even the obituaries, reminders of the most salient drama of every day, provided a soothing sense of perspective.

Algorithms, by narrowing the content we see, should mitigate the negative effects of information acceleration Lehmann described, but they don’t. “The algorithms help you find content in the effectively infinite amount of stuff that’s out there,” he conceded. “But they’re problematic for the following reason: They’re not working to find the things that are most relevant for you to know about, rather they’re working to show you the content that will keep you engaged with the app for as long as possible. Things that keep you engaged are typically negative and sensational, causing anxiety.” The personalized news aggregators on my phone, judging by the number of articles about “extreme turbulence” I am served, do not discriminate between stressful-engaging and interesting-engaging.

The engagement that Lehmann was referring to might last for the same amount of time it takes to read a long profile of a presidential candidate in print—I could easily spend 30 minutes doomscrolling through chunks of election intel—but it isn’t necessarily as substantive. Ladislao Salmerón, a psychologist at the University of Valencia, studied the differences in comprehension between reading on a digital platform and reading in print. He and his colleagues found that we don’t synthesize information as well when we’re reading on our phones; reading on a phone, they saw, also doesn’t help us become better readers the way reading in print does (this is especially true of adolescents).

When I asked Salmerón to speculate on a connection between lower levels of comprehension and higher stress, he suggested that the anxiety that I feel when I read on my phone might be due to two factors. The first is other people’s opinions: being exposed to other readers’ comments on a piece of information, as one typically is on social media, changes how we interpret that information, and if those comments are extreme or impolite, that can influence us to take a more extreme position on the subject. And if I see that someone else finds information distressing, I might feel more distressed by it than I otherwise would. The second factor is the sheer effort required to read something on it a screen. Reading digitally is, Salmerón explained, cognitively and visually taxing. I feel energized after reading the paper in the morning, but I feel exhausted after 20 minutes of reading on my phone.

Still, I don’t see myself completely pivoting to newspapers, Johann Hari-style, ahead of the upcoming election. Perhaps because it isn’t happening in the thick of a global pandemic, it hasn’t been as difficult to manage my attention during this race. Besides which, not all online commentary adds stress: I would miss the jokes.
